I thought the best reward for hard work would be having nothing difficult left to do. Then I earned a chance I had wanted for years and found harder work waiting. The reward was getting to spend my effort on a problem I had chosen.
I used to think I needed to become more competitive. I could already tell you who had beaten me and by how much. What I couldn't tell you was what they had done differently. Curiosity did more for my next attempt than resentment ever managed.
The trouble with trying to prove that you can do everything alone is how little you learn about working with other people. I thought accepting support would make the achievement smaller. It let me try something too difficult for my previous definition of strength.
I thought knowing myself meant having a fixed answer about what I was good at. That made every unfamiliar task feel like a threat to an old conclusion. I know myself better now that some of my answers have dates and a few have changed.
I had become skilled at planning how to recover from failure. What I kept overlooking was how to spend the hours after a modest success. Those were the hours I usually wasted celebrating a good beginning as if the rest had already taken care of itself.
A quiet day can feel empty after a long stretch of proving yourself. I used to fill it with any task that would let me call myself busy. Learning to leave an hour unclaimed showed me which ambitions came back without being called.
It is tempting to want credit for what you intended to sacrifice. I had mentally given up weekends and comfort many times before the work actually asked. The only sacrifice that changed the result was the one I still agreed to when I learned its exact price.
I used to choose a goal by imagining the best day of achieving it. Now I also try to imagine a dull week halfway there. If I can still find something worth doing in that week, the goal has a much better chance of surviving its announcement.
I could always find time to explain why I hadn't made progress. The explanation changed with the month and the audience, but the time I spent giving it stayed surprisingly consistent. The first practice slot I could reliably keep was the one those explanations gave back.
A useful rival can move on and still leave you better at your work. I knew mine had become a distraction when their bad day felt as satisfying as my good one. From then on I kept score using something their bad luck could never improve.
Confidence was easier to rebuild once I stopped asking whether I believed in myself as a whole person. I could believe that I would make one call or finish one page because I had done it before. A specific promise gave trust somewhere small enough to begin.
A habit built entirely around a reward has to survive the days the reward arrives late. I spent those days wondering whether the work had been wasted. It got easier to return when I could name one part of the practice worth having even without the result.
My plans used to assign the hardest work to a future version of me with unlimited energy. I was kinder to myself today than I ever was to the person waking up tomorrow. Growing up meant giving both of us a fair share of the difficult part.
There is an advantage to admitting you are inexperienced while the stakes are small. Someone can correct you before a misunderstanding has had time to become a habit. I spent a long time trying to look ready for chances that honesty would have prepared me to take.
When the work matters deeply, a poor attempt can feel almost disrespectful to the idea. I used to protect important ideas by leaving them untouched. Giving one my present skill finally taught me the skills I thought I had to earn before I was allowed to begin.
